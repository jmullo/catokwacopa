# The Catokwacopa cryptograms (1875): readings

Two encrypted advertisements signed "W." appeared in the personal column of
the London *Standard* (*Evening Standard*) on 8 May and 20 May 1875. The
second ends in plain English: *"This will be intelligible if read in
connection with my communication published in this column on the 8th inst."*

**How they work.** Each group of the 8 May ad pairs with the group in the
same position in the 20 May ad. The two are halves of one short phrase: the
phrase's letters are shared out between the ads in their original order, and
many letters are simply left out (abbreviation). Numbers are split the same
way (`18` from 20 May + `53` from 8 May = 1853). The decoded text reads like
notes by an Oxford man about 1853–1856.

**Notation** (line-by-line table):
- Letters in [brackets] are printed in neither ad and have to be supplied.
  A whole word in [brackets] is left out entirely.
- "…" in a reading marks a part not yet read. In the Alternatives column,
  "…" stands for the part that is the same as in the main reading.
- *Status*:
  - **solid:** the letters (or digits) force the reading; or they fit it
    and a language model prefers it by a wide margin over every
    alternative (line 10); or a dated source confirms it (line 11);
  - **likely:** the letters fit well and the meaning fits the surrounding
    lines;
  - **possible:** fits, but rivals remain;
  - **open:** no satisfactory reading yet.

  A status followed by words in parentheses applies only to that part of
  the line.

## Interpretation (a guess, not a reading)

W. seems to be putting an old grievance on record. He sat the Hertford
Latin scholarship in March 1854. Candidates apparently entered under
mottoes, presumably so the examiners would not know whose work was whose,
and W. chose *Odes* 1.6.9. But mottoes were "privately discussed in
college". Conington, whose
lectures W. attended, told his pupils to add a second motto, and Conington
was one of the three examiners. In the examination room a Balliol man
opposite muttered *Ars Poetica* 138, a jibe that only makes sense if he knew
W.'s motto. The scholarship went to a Balliol man. Later W. told Shirley
(line 24). Twenty years on he set it all down as a "dying declaration": a
solemn statement of the truth, literal or figurative. The opening episode
of the stolen cap (1853) may introduce the same rival, but that is
uncertain.

## People and context

- **John Conington** (1825–1869) was a Fellow of University College from
  1848 and became the first Corpus Professor of Latin in 1854. He was a
  noted editor of Virgil and translator of Horace.
- **Benjamin Jowett** (1817–1893) of Balliol became Regius Professor of
  Greek in 1855. In the same year his commentary on St Paul's Epistles drew
  charges of unorthodoxy, and he was required to re-subscribe to the
  Thirty-Nine Articles.
- **Walter Waddington Shirley** (1828–1866), a Rugby man, was elected a
  Fellow of Wadham in June 1853 and was its tutor from 1855.
- The **Hertford Scholarship** is a university prize for Latin. In the
  1850s it was elected annually in Lent Term by three examiners, from
  undergraduates within two years of matriculation (*Oxford University
  Calendar* 1854). Oxford prize compositions were submitted under a motto;
  lines 12–17 suggest that candidates for this examination used mottoes
  too.
- **The 1854 examination** began on Thursday 23 March 1854, with 30
  candidates (*Morning Post*, 24 March 1854). The examiners
  were George Butler (Exeter), **John Conington** (University) and Edwin
  Palmer (Balliol). On Monday 27 March they elected F. B. M. Montgomerie, a
  commoner of Balliol, and named Robinson Ellis (Balliol) and Mr Cattlow
  (St John's) as eminent (*Ecclesiastical Gazette*, 1854). Conington, whose
  lectures W. attended (line 8) and who "told us to add a second motto"
  (line 15), was therefore one of the examiners.
- The winners for these years were all Balliol men: 1853 William Lambert
  Newman, **1854 F. B. M. Montgomerie**, 1855 C. S. C. Bowen, 1856 Reginald
  Broughton. Jowett (1837) and Conington (1844) had won it themselves.
- A **dying declaration** is, in English law, a statement made by someone
  who believes death is imminent; it can be admitted as evidence.
- Who "W." was is not known.
  - Thomas William Jex-Blake (University College, a pupil of Conington,
    runner-up in the Lent 1853 Hertford, ordained 1856) fits much of the
    text. But his Hertford attempt was in March 1853, and by 1854 he was past
    the two-year limit, so he cannot have sat the 1854 examination.
  - Main Swete Alexander Walrond (Balliol, matriculated March 1853, later
    vicar of St Lawrence Jewry) fits the dates, but died in 1899.
  - **Horace Waddington** (c. 1834 – 26 August 1930) fits most of the
    profile:
    - Rugby; matriculated at University College (Conington's college) in
      May 1853, so his first term was Summer 1853;
    - Univ scholar from 1854, First in Classical Moderations 1855 and in
      Classics 1857;
    - later a barrister and H.M. Inspector of Schools;
    - his father, Major-General Charles Waddington, was the brother of
      W. W. Shirley's mother, Maria Waddington, so "told Shirley" (line 24)
      would mean telling his cousin.

    Against him: he died in 1930 aged 96, so "dying declaration" would have
    to be figurative. He left a legacy to Rugby School for a Greek and Latin
    exhibition at Oxford.
  - **William Spencer Ollivant** (1835–1876), son of the Bishop of Llandaff:
    Rugby, Christ Church 1853, barrister. He was ill in 1875–76 and died at
    Chester in October 1876, which fits a literal dying declaration. He has
    no known link to Conington or Shirley.
  - The newspapers named only three of the 30 candidates of 1854, so
    neither man can be placed in the examination room.

## How this was done

The analysis on this page was carried out with Claude Opus 5.5. Claude
wrote and ran the search and checking programs and proposed readings;
every reading here was then tested mechanically against the printed
letters.
- **Letter fit:** each reading is checked against the letters of both ads in
  order, and its abbreviations are compared with W.'s habits in the
  accepted lines.
- **Language model as a second judge:** a small open language model,
  Qwen3.5-2B-Base, ran locally. It scores how natural a candidate line
  reads in the text around it, and chooses among readings that fit the
  letters equally well.
- **Sources:** dates and people were checked in the printed sources listed
  below. Where a reading rests on them (line 11), the table says so.
