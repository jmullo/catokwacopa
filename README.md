# The Catokwacopa cryptograms (1875): readings

Two encrypted advertisements headed "W." appeared in the personal column of
the London *Standard* (*Evening Standard*) on 8 May and 20 May 1875. The
second ends in plain English: *"This will be intelligible if read in
connection with my communication published in this column on the 8th inst."*
"W." may be the writer's own initial or that of the person the ads are
addressed to; this page says "the writer" for the author.

The decoded text reads like notes by an Oxford man about 1853–1856. About
two thirds of it is read with confidence; five lines are still open.

## How the cipher works

Each group of the 8 May ad pairs with the group in the same position in the
20 May ad. The two are halves of one short phrase: the phrase's letters are
shared out between the ads in their original order, and many letters are
simply left out (abbreviation). Numbers are split the same way (`18` from
20 May + `53` from 8 May = 1853).

There is no key. The writer split each phrase by ear, making the 8 May groups
pronounceable (CA-TO-KWA-CO-PA, MO-PRE-DIS-CO) and putting the rest in the
20 May ad. A reading has to be guessed and then checked against the letters.
The writer does have clear habits:
- **Letters keep their order** in each ad.
- **The two halves are about the same length.**
- **The first letter of each word is printed**, usually in the 8 May ad.
- **The letter after a gap is usually in the 20 May ad**, and so is the
  last printed letter of a word.
- **Letters are left out from the middle of words**, rarely from the end:
  CON[I]NGT[O]N, LEC[T]UR[E]S. An E just before the last letter is often
  dropped: GARD[E]N, SHIRL[E]Y.
- **Whole words are rarely left out:** [OF] SATIRES is the only clear case.

## Notation

- In the line-by-line table, letters in [brackets] are printed in neither ad
  and have to be supplied; a whole word in [brackets] is left out entirely.
- "…" marks a part not yet read.
- *Status*: **solid** (the letters force it, or a dated source confirms
  it), **likely** (fits well, no rival), **possible** (fits, but rivals fit
  as well), **open** (no whole reading yet). Where only part of a line is
  read, the status in parentheses applies to that part.

## Interpretation (a guess, not a reading)

The writer sets out three dated episodes, like evidence, and calls them a "dying
declaration": a solemn statement for the record.

1. **Summer term 1853.** At a college party the writer's cap was taken and an old,
   broken one left in its place. Someone met him in the garden and twice
   told him "your cap is honest …". Who, and why it matters, is unread.
2. **March 1854, the Hertford.** This is the core. The writer chose a motto,
   *conamur tenues grandia*, "we, slight ones, attempt great things". The
   mottoes were discussed in college, and Conington, who was one of the
   three examiners, told his pupils to add a second one; the writer added *Qui fit*,
   the opening of the *Satires*: "how is it that no one is content with his
   lot?" A Balliol man opposite seems to have muttered *Ars Poetica* 138,
   "what will this promiser bring forth worthy of such a gaping mouth?", a
   jibe that only works if he knew the writer's motto. So the examination was not
   anonymous, and the scholarship went to a Balliol man.
3. **1855–6, the Hody examination.** Wadham's Hody exhibitioners were
   examined each term, by the rules, by the Regius Professor of Greek: from
   1855 Jowett, whose lectures the writer attended. Robert Griffith was placed
   first and the writer second. The writer told Shirley. Both teachers' lines may end "… pupil",
   and the last line may say the writer "followed both masters": Conington and
   Jowett, who were at odds from 1855.

The Hody line points to the writer being a Wadham man who held a Hody
Greek exhibition in 1855–6. If "W." is the writer's initial, the best fit
is **Robert Holdsworth Walker**: he came up in spring 1853 (so summer 1853 was his first term), held that
exhibition for exactly 1855–6, the only one of its six holders whose name
begins with W, and was a mathematician while Shirley was Wadham's
mathematical tutor. Two Griffiths held the exhibition then, which would
explain why the writer wrote out "Robt".

Two things count against Walker. He had no honours in classics, yet the
writer comes second only to Griffith. And before 1854 Conington taught as Greek
lecturer of University College, whose lectures were closed to other
colleges, so a Wadham man could have heard him only as a private pupil; no
record says either way. Both points suit **Horace Waddington** of
University College better: Conington's college, a strong Latinist, and
Shirley's first cousin. But he held no Wadham exhibition, so he does not
fit the Hody lines. If "dying declaration" is meant literally, the one man
with a W. name of that year who was dying in 1875 is **Ogle Richard
Wintle** of Lincoln College (died November 1875 "after five years' illness"); nothing links
him to the story.

If "W." is instead the person addressed, the writer can be any of the
Wadham Hody Greek exhibitioners of 1855–6. The closest fit is then
**Ernest Ridsdale Ellaby** of Wadham: a classicist (second class in
Moderations in 1855, the term Griffith took a first), later a Fellow of
Wadham, a barrister and a verse translator of Dante. In October 1875 he
repeatedly asked a London magistrate to receive a written "declaration"
that he was being drugged, and was found to be suffering from delusions;
he spent his last years in care and died in 1896. A man who believed he
was being poisoned might well call his statement a "dying declaration".
Nothing yet links him to Conington, and this too is a guess.

Why the writer published in 1875 is unknown. By then Griffith, Shirley and
Conington, who could have confirmed his story, were dead; Conington's chair
was held by Edwin Palmer, the Balliol examiner of 1854; and the 1854
winner's family lived at St Leonards-on-Sea, a few streets from the school
Walker had run since 1874.

## People and context

- **John Conington** (1825–1869), Fellow of University College, translator
  of Horace; Greek lecturer of his college in 1853–54, one of the three
  examiners of the 1854 Hertford, and Professor of Latin from that summer.
- **Benjamin Jowett** (1817–1893) of Balliol, Regius Professor of Greek
  from 1855. From 1855 he and Conington were at odds, so the two men whose
  lectures the writer attended stood on opposite sides.
- **W. W. Shirley** (1828–1866), Fellow of Wadham, tutor and mathematical
  lecturer there from 1855; a first cousin of Horace Waddington.
- **The Hertford Scholarship** is the University's Latin scholarship. The
  1854 examination began on 23 March with 30 candidates; on 27 March it went
  to F. B. M. Montgomerie of Balliol. The examiners were George Butler,
  Conington and Edwin Palmer of Balliol, who held Conington's chair of
  Latin from 1870. Every winner from 1853 to 1856 was a Balliol man.
- **Baliol** is the period spelling of Balliol College.
- **Hody exhibitions** at Wadham College: four for Hebrew and six for Greek,
  their holders "examined once in every Term by the Regius Professor of
  those languages".
- **Robert Griffith** of Wadham came up in spring 1853, held a Hody Greek
  exhibition 1853–59 and won the Ireland, the University's Greek
  scholarship, in 1856. He died in 1863.
- **Robert Holdsworth Walker** (1834–1904) of Wadham: admitted March 1853,
  Hody Greek exhibitioner 1855–56, First in mathematics, deacon 1859;
  principal of Barham House school, St Leonards-on-Sea, from 1874.
- **Horace Waddington** (c. 1834–1930) of University College: came up in
  May 1853, elected Univ scholar two days before the 1854 Hertford, First
  in Classics; later an inspector of schools.
- A **dying declaration** is, in English law, a statement made by someone
  who believes death is near; it can be admitted as evidence.
