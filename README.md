# The Catokwacopa cryptograms (1875): readings

Two encrypted advertisements signed "W." appeared in the personal column of
the London *Standard* (*Evening Standard*) on 8 May and 20 May 1875. The
second ends in plain English: *"This will be intelligible if read in
connection with my communication published in this column on the 8th inst."*

The decoded text reads like notes by an Oxford man about 1853–1856.

## How the cipher works

Each group of the 8 May ad pairs with the group in the same position in the
20 May ad. The two are halves of one short phrase: the phrase's letters are
shared out between the ads in their original order, and many letters are
simply left out (abbreviation). Numbers are split the same way (`18` from
20 May + `53` from 8 May = 1853).

**No fixed method, only habits.** W. followed no fixed rule for which
letters go into which ad, or which are left out; often a single word
switches back and forth between the ads. He seems to have split each phrase
by ear, making the 8 May groups pronounceable, like invented words
(CA-TO-KWA-CO-PA, MO-PRE-DIS-CO), and putting the remaining letters in the
20 May ad. So there is no key that turns the ads back into text. A reading
has to be guessed and then checked against the letters.

W. does have a few clear habits:
- **Letters keep their order:** the letters of each ad appear in the phrase
  in the order they are printed.
- **The two halves are about the same length.**
- **The first letter of each word is printed**, usually in the 8 May ad.
- **The letter after a gap is usually in the 20 May ad**, and so is the
  last printed letter of a word.
- **Letters are left out from the middle of words**, rarely from the end:
  CON[I]NGT[O]N, LEC[T]UR[E]S. An E just before the last letter is often
  dropped: GARD[E]N, SHIRL[E]Y.
- **Whole words are rarely left out:** [OF] SATIRES is the only clear case.

These habits rule out most guesses, but where several phrases fit the
letters they cannot decide between them. That is why some lines below are
still *possible* or *open*. W.'s intended reader knew the events and did
not need more.

## Notation

For the line-by-line table:
- Letters in [brackets] are printed in neither ad and have to be supplied.
  A whole word in [brackets] is left out entirely.
- "…" in a reading marks a part not yet read. In the Alternatives column,
  "…" stands for the part that is the same as in the main reading.
- *Status*:
  - **solid:** the letters (or digits) force the reading; or they fit it
    and a language model prefers it by a wide margin over every
    alternative (lines 10, 15); or a dated source confirms it (line 11);
  - **likely:** the letters fit well and the meaning fits the surrounding
    lines;
  - **possible:** fits, but rivals remain;
  - **open:** no satisfactory reading yet.

  A status followed by words in parentheses applies only to that part of
  the line.

## Interpretation (a guess, not a reading)

W. seems to be putting an old grievance on record. He sat the Hertford Latin
scholarship in March 1854. Candidates apparently entered under mottoes,
presumably so the examiners would not know whose work was whose, and W.
chose *Odes* 1.6.9. But mottoes were "privately discussed in college".
Conington, whose lectures W. attended, told his pupils to add a second
motto, and Conington was one of the three examiners. Conington was not yet a
professor at the time (he took up the Latin chair only that autumn), so
whatever teaching W. had from him came from a Fellow of University College.
That points to W. being a University College man, or close to the college.
In the examination room a Balliol man opposite muttered *Ars Poetica* 138, a
jibe that only makes sense if he knew W.'s motto. The scholarship went to a
Balliol man. Later W. told Shirley (line 24). Twenty years on he set it all
down as a "dying declaration": a solemn statement of the truth, literal or
figurative. The opening episode of the stolen cap (1853) may introduce the
same rival, but that is uncertain. The later lines move on to 1855–6: an
examination (lines 21–22) and Jowett's lectures (line 25). Jowett's first
course as professor began in the same term in which the Vice-Chancellor made
him sign the Thirty-Nine Articles again. Line 23 may end with the word
"summoner", which would fit that episode, but the rest of the line is
unread.

## People and context

- **John Conington** (1825–1869) was a Fellow of University College from
  1848 and became the first Corpus Professor of Latin in 1854. He was a
  noted editor of Virgil and translator of Horace.
  - The Latin chair was created by a statute of Convocation on 14 March
    1854, nine days before the Hertford examination began, and the first
    election was held on 14 June 1854. So while Conington examined the
    Hertford, the new chair was pending, and he went on to win it.
    - He took up the chair in Michaelmas Term 1854; until then he lived in
    University College. Throughout his career, in "each successive
    generation of undergraduates" he would "attach to himself one or two of
    the most promising scholars".
  - In the Long Vacation of 1854 he went through a sudden religious crisis,
    and came out of it resolved to make religion "the sole governing
    principle of his life".

  (Memoir by H. J. S. Smith and editor's notes in Conington's
  *Miscellaneous Writings*, 1872.)
- **Benjamin Jowett** (1817–1893) of Balliol became Regius Professor of
  Greek in 1855.
  - In the same year his commentary on St Paul's Epistles drew charges of
    unorthodoxy. In Michaelmas Term 1855 the Vice-Chancellor, R. L. Cotton
    (Provost of Worcester), summoned him to sign the Thirty-Nine Articles
    again; G. V. Cox dates the complaint to the beginning of December
    (*Recollections of Oxford*, 2nd ed., 1870).
    - His first course as professor, on Plato's *Republic*, was given in
    Balliol hall and stayed well attended for years; by 1865 the hall was
    filled with undergraduates from various colleges.
  - From 1855 he and Conington were at odds. His biographers write that
    "the Latin and Greek Professors were not in sympathy": Conington, after
    his religious change, was "genuinely scandalized" by Jowett's doubts
    about accepted rules of Greek scholarship. They also
    mention Jowett's "old prejudice" against Conington, which he later
    acknowledged. So the two
    men whose lectures W. attended (lines 8 and 25) stood on opposite sides
    by 1855–6.

  (E. Abbott and L. Campbell, *The Life and Letters of Benjamin Jowett*,
  1897, vol. 1 pp. 236–241 and 249, vol. 2.)
- **Walter Waddington Shirley** (1828–1866), a Rugby man, was elected a
  Fellow of Wadham in June 1853 and was its tutor from 1855.
- The **Hertford Scholarship** is a university prize for Latin. In the
  1850s a scholar was elected annually in Lent Term by three examiners,
  from undergraduates within two years of matriculation (*Oxford University
  Calendar* 1854). Oxford prize compositions were submitted under a motto;
  lines 12–17 suggest that candidates for this examination used mottoes
  too.
- **The 1854 examination** began on Thursday 23 March 1854, with 30
  candidates (*Morning Post*, 24 March 1854). The examiners
  were George Butler (Exeter), **John Conington** (University) and Edwin
  Palmer (Balliol). On Monday 27 March they elected F. B. M. Montgomerie, a
  commoner of Balliol, and named Robinson Ellis (Balliol) and J. S. Catlow
  (St John's) as eminent (*Ecclesiastical Gazette*, 1854). Conington, whose
  lectures W. attended (line 8) and who "told us to add a second motto"
  (line 15), was therefore one of the examiners.
- The winners for these years were all Balliol men: 1853 William Lambert
  Newman, **1854 F. B. M. Montgomerie**, 1855 C. S. C. Bowen, 1856 Reginald
  Broughton. Jowett (1837) and Conington (1844) had won it themselves.
- A **dying declaration** is, in English law, a statement made by someone
  who believes death is imminent; it can be admitted as evidence.
- Who "W." was is not known.
  - Thomas William Jex-Blake (University College, a pupil of Conington,
    runner-up in the Lent 1853 Hertford, ordained 1856) fits much of the
    text. But his Hertford attempt was in March 1853, and by 1854 he was past
    the two-year limit, so he cannot have sat the 1854 examination.
  - Main Swete Alexander Walrond (Balliol, matriculated March 1853, later
    vicar of St Lawrence Jewry) fits the dates, but died in 1899.
  - **Horace Waddington** (c. 1834 – 26 August 1930) fits most of the
    profile:
    - Rugby; matriculated at University College (Conington's college) in
      May 1853, so his first term was Summer 1853;
    - Univ scholar from 1854, First in Classical Moderations 1855 and in
      Classics 1857;
    - later a barrister and H.M. Inspector of Schools;
    - his father, Major-General Charles Waddington, was the brother of
      W. W. Shirley's mother, Maria Waddington, so "told Shirley" (line 24)
      would mean telling his cousin.

    Against him: he died in 1930 aged 96, so "dying declaration" would have
    to be figurative. He left a legacy to Rugby School for a Greek and Latin
    exhibition at Oxford.
  - **William Spencer Ollivant** (1835–1876), son of the Bishop of Llandaff:
    Rugby, Christ Church 1853, barrister. He had been "for some time out of health"
    when he died at Chester in October 1876 (*South Wales Daily News*,
    5 October 1876), which fits a literal dying declaration. He has no known
    personal link to Conington or Shirley.
  - The newspapers named only three of the 30 candidates of 1854, so
    none of these men can be placed in the examination room.

## How this was done

The analysis on this page was carried out with Claude Opus 5.5. Claude
wrote and ran the search and checking programs and proposed readings;
every reading here was then tested mechanically against the printed
letters.
- **Letter fit:** each reading is checked against the letters of both ads in
  order, and its abbreviations are compared with W.'s habits in the
  accepted lines.
- **Language model as a second judge:** a small open language model,
  Qwen3.5-2B-Base, ran locally. It scores how natural a candidate line
  reads in the text around it, and chooses among readings that fit the
  letters equally well.
- **Sources:** dates and people were checked in the printed sources listed
  below. Where a reading rests on them (line 11), the table says so.
